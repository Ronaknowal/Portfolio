## Scoped teaching progression and reasoning

1. **A direction is a point, not a pair of universal coordinates.** Begin with a compass direction on a circle. A single angle wraps; two open angle charts cover the circle, with transition functions differing by 2π on separate overlap components. Define a smooth finite-dimensional manifold through locally Euclidean, Hausdorff/second-countable space and compatible charts, briefly recalling why open neighborhoods permit differentiation. The sphere pole is a chart failure, not a singular surface point. Abstract manifolds do not require the particular pictured embedding. A crossing of two lines fails to be a one-dimensional manifold at its crossing; do not use a cone as an unqualified topological counterexample.
2. **A tangent vector is an instantaneous motion.** Differentiate the constraint x·x=1 to get x·v=0, distinguish the tangent vector space through the origin from its drawn affine copy anchored at x, and derive a coordinate basis from an embedding F and its Jacobian J. Define dF(v), the chain rule and scalar df(v). Tangent dimension is intrinsic. Explain full-rank regular constraints locally; arbitrary constraints can have singular levels. Use polar F(r,θ) and the sphere's two angle coordinates away from poles.
3. **Choose how to measure a local move.** A smooth SPD metric G measures tangent-vector pairings; induced geometry gives G=JᵀJ. Derive polar diag(1,r²) and sphere diag(R²,R²sin²θ). The latter is positive definite on its valid chart. Separate a coordinate transformation G_new=SᵀGS from changing the physical metric. Implement the incoming shear example: S=[[1,1],[0,1]], differential coefficients [2,1], G=[[1,1],[1,2]], gradient components [3,−1], reconstructing [2,−1]. Derive G grad=df_coefficients from dual pairing and steepest change by metric Cauchy–Schwarz. For a sphere with the induced Euclidean metric, separately derive projected ambient gradient; an arbitrary chosen metric does not justify the same projector.
4. **Accumulate local measurements.** Derive length as the integral of metric speed and its invariance under an orientation-preserving reparameterization; energy depends on parameter speed, with fixed-time Cauchy–Schwarz relating energy and length. Define distance as the infimum over piecewise smooth joining curves. A round sphere gives R times the shorter central angle, versus its chord; at antipodes the minimizing direction is nonunique. Explain metric volume sqrt(det G), the sphere area element and equal-angle versus equal-area sampling. A deterministic band-area example makes the pole bias visible; no sampling graphic is presented as observed data.
5. **Follow locally straight motion, then optimize.** Introduce the intrinsic geodesic condition before its coordinate formula. Sphere constant-speed great circles have normal ambient acceleration and zero tangent acceleration. Derive Exp_x(v)=cos(|v|)x+sinc(|v|)v on the unit sphere, local Log away from the antipode, and normalized retraction with identity first derivative. Compare exact traveled angle |v| with retraction angle atan(|v|); a long geodesic need not minimize distance. Exp may have only a local domain on a general incomplete manifold. A connected complete finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold has minimizing joining geodesics, but this says neither uniqueness nor that every long geodesic minimizes. Preserve and explain the original program at this point. A valid update does not establish descent; large steps and stationary maxima are part of the diagnosis.
6. **Differentiate a moving basis.** Define vector fields and the need to compare tangent spaces. A connection obeys linearity in the direction and the product rule in the field. The Levi-Civita connection is metric-compatible and torsion-free. Derive the coordinate coefficient formula from metric compatibility plus symmetry (show intermediate lowered-index identity), not as unexplained notation. In the plane, Γʳ_θθ=−r and Γθ_rθ=Γθ_θr=1/r. For the straight Cartesian line (t,b), compute r'', θ'' and show both polar geodesic equations cancel. Nonzero coefficients can describe a rotating basis in a flat space. Differentiate scalar gradients for the Hessian; on the sphere show the normal-curvature correction and a concrete Rayleigh stationary-point test.
7. **Transport and curvature.** Parallel transport solves covariant derivative zero along a specified path and preserves inner products for this metric-compatible connection. Use actual great-circle rotations of a tangent arrow and an orthogonal triangle N→A→B→N. Derive the 90° final rotation for the right-angle example, reverse-route sign and retraced-route identity. A longitude wedge of angle φ has area R²φ, and its transport angle is φ under the chosen orientation. Define the curvature operator with a declared sign convention, explaining the Lie bracket locally and commuting coordinate fields. Derive K=−a''/a for metric du²+a(u)²dv²; compare plane/polar, cylinder, sphere and a hyperbolic chart. A cylinder is bent in 3D but intrinsically flat locally. One-dimensional circle Riemann curvature is zero; it must not be confused with extrinsic curve curvature. Explain sectional curvature, Ricci contraction and scalar curvature as further summaries, with scalar=2K only on surfaces. A constant-curvature Jacobi/circumference comparison is calculated theory, not a finite observed displacement or a learned-data result.
8. **Apply a declared geometry.** Resolve the old retrieval exercise: for nonzero unit-normalized vectors cosine, chord and spherical angle give the same ranking through monotone transforms, but not identical values/averages; raw Euclidean distance includes magnitude. Compare extrinsic and intrinsic circle means for angles0°,0°,90° (26.565° versus30°) and the antipodal ambiguity. For SPD matrices, distinguish the open flat Euclidean cone from log-Euclidean and affine-invariant choices. Derive the diagonal multiplicative path and explain a general symmetric-eigendecomposition implementation of the affine-invariant maps/distance; the same pair can have different chosen midpoints. For the Bernoulli Fisher metric, reuse the earlier Second-Order Methods definitions rather than duplicate its optimizer lab: derive arc coordinate u=2asin(sqrt p) and finite boundary distance, and restate tangent invariance versus coordinate-dependent finite Euler updates. Hyperbolic geometry supplies an explicit vertical distance/curvature example, not a performance ranking for embeddings. Rotation/frame/group and neural manifold applications receive reasoned links rather than an unproved claim that any learned embedding recovers a physical manifold.
9. **Complete a checked workflow.** Minimize xᵀAx on a unit sphere, with a fully specified small symmetric matrix and initial point. Derive tangent gradient2(Ax−fx), check it along tangent curves, perform normalized-retraction backtracking with finite step/iteration limits, record norm, objective, gradient and status, and independently compare the final Rayleigh value to the smallest eigenvalue. Use a changed rotated matrix in practice. A small gradient is stationarity, and a starting eigenvector can be a maximum or saddle; the Hessian/known eigenspectrum diagnoses that limit. State the precise convergence claim actually established by the fixture, not a universal optimizer guarantee.
10. **Independent transfer and follow-on owners.** Changed chart seams, tangent constraints, shears/metric costs, a latitude path and area band, cut-locus counterexamples, polar cancellation, triangle reversal, curvature sign, means, SPD maps and the changed optimization fixture. Each task has an optional hint and explained answer or full runnable accepted result. Keep the core derivations visible; optional details serve additional depth rather than conceal required explanations.

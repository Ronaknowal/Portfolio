## Learner finish line and teaching route

The continuing object is a square with four distinguishable positions and movable labels/readings. The learner should leave able to specify a symmetry contract, reason about composition, distinguish the transformations from the objects transformed, count genuinely different configurations, and construct/test an equivariant finite linear operation. Exact enumeration supports small examples; general claims receive proofs with their hypotheses.

| Stage and hurdle | Teaching mechanism, worked route and assessed outcome |
| --- | --- |
| 1. What can change while an object remains the same? | Separate square geometry, fixed position labels and movable sensor readings. A geometric symmetry preserves distances between positions; a particular colored configuration need not remain unchanged. Rotate a labeled square and compare one-step and two-step routes before any axioms. Explicitly distinguish a transformation from its result. |
| 2. Which way do operations compose? | Let `r` be a counterclockwise quarter-turn and `s` reflection in the horizontal axis. Use **gh = apply h, then g** throughout prose, matrix products, diagrams, table headers and code. Trace vertex 1 under rs to vertex 0, and under sr to vertex 2. Derive r⁴=e, s²=e and sr=r⁻¹s from the actual positions. Eight unique maps are `r^k s^b`, not eight strings selected by appearance. |
| 3. Why are these a group? | Introduce set, closed binary operation, associativity, identity and inverses as guarantees for composing/reversing moves. Prove associativity of function composition by evaluating an arbitrary input; do not confuse it with swapping order. Derive inverse uniqueness and cancellation using the laws. Show closure failure for only {e,r}, inverse failure for nonzero integers under multiplication, and lost information in a noninvertible clipping map. A Latin-looking table is not itself an associativity proof. |
| 4. How do small groups sit inside larger ones? | Powers generate cyclic subgroups. Derive element order and explain why “same number of elements” does not establish isomorphism (C4 has an order-four element, C2×C2 does not). Subgroup test includes nonempty and closure under gh⁻¹. Explain left cosets as translated copies of a subgroup, prove equal-or-disjoint and equal size, then obtain finite Lagrange divisibility. Distinguish a Cayley graph's group-element nodes from an action graph's configuration nodes. Supply a small generated-move diagram and exact coset table. |
| 5. Why can eight moves give only two results? | Define a left action e·x=x and (gh)·x=g·(h·x). Identity and inverses make each action map bijective. Build orbit and stabilizer for a vertex, a one-mark coloring, an opposite-pair coloring and a constant coloring. Prove stabilizer is a subgroup and the coset-to-orbit bijection, with well-definedness/injectivity/surjectivity; hence |G|=|orbit(x)|·|stabilizer(x)| for finite G. A group action may be nonfaithful; its kernel fixes **every** x, unlike one stabilizer. |
| 6. Count configurations without double counting | Begin with all 16 binary vertex colorings; inspect their six orbits, including adjacent versus opposite marked pairs. Explain why 16/8 is wrong. Derive finite Burnside counting by counting pairs (g,x) with g·x=x in two ways and using the orbit–stabilizer result. Explain a fixed coloring is constant on each permutation cycle, giving q^(number of cycles). The changed three-color task distinguishes rotations only (24 orbits) from rotations/reflections (21), avoiding the misleading binary coincidence. |
| 7. What does a structure-preserving summary forget? | Define homomorphism through a commuting composition square; identity/inverse preservation follows from its law. Define image/kernel/isomorphism and prove the kernel is normal. The square's orientation sign is ±1; its kernel is the rotation subgroup. Use this concrete quotient before the general construction: (gN)(hN)=ghN is independent of representatives exactly for normal N. Exhibit failure for H={e,s}, where eH=sH but rH≠srH. Explain G/ker(phi)→image(phi) with its short complete well-defined/bijection proof. Connect to the already-known partition concept without pretending all partitions admit multiplication. |
| 8. How does symmetry constrain a calculation? | A real linear representation maps G into invertible linear maps and preserves multiplication. Link the 2×2 geometric matrices to 4×4 permutation matrices; they are different actions of the same group. Prove the permutation action on data is `(P_g x)[j]=x[g⁻¹j]`, since the old value at i moves to gi. Distinguish invariance F(P_gx)=F(x) from equivariance F(P_gx)=Q_gF(x); input and output representations can differ. Derive W P_g=Q_g W for linear maps, not just one successful input. |
| 9. Construct and audit a useful symmetry-respecting map | Four identical position channels motivate weights tied by self/neighbor/opposite relations. Derive the three-parameter D4-equivariant family, contrasting its four-parameter rotation-only circulant family. Work one entire vector example, then repair an arbitrary weight matrix by finite averaging `Wbar=(1/|G|) sum P_g⁻¹ W P_g`. Prove commutation by reindexing and idempotence. For these orthogonal permutation actions, optionally prove this is the Frobenius orthogonal projection, with the residual orthogonal to every tied map. Explain pooled means as invariant and pointwise nonlinearities as permutation-equivariant; coordinatewise ReLU does **not** commute with every rotation/reflection matrix. Distinguish mathematical weight sharing from trained accuracy, finite sums from Haar integration, and exact grid actions from arbitrary rotated/resampled images. |
| 10. Arithmetic structures beyond groups; independent synthesis | Optional ring/field branch: additive abelian group plus associative multiplication and distributivity; this lesson explicitly uses unital rings and distinguishes commutativity. Explain Z/nZ addition/multiplication, units and zero divisors. Derive why an inverse is equivalent to gcd(a,n)=1 using a locally explained Bezout identity or an elementary finite permutation argument for prime n. Compare 2x=4 modulo 5 and 6; cancelling 2 loses a valid solution modulo 6. A field requires 0≠1 and every nonzero element invertible, not just addition. Explain that finite fields are not all integers modulo their size. Conclude with a scoped map to ideals/polynomial quotient fields, representations, Lie groups and specialist applications; mark these as further learning, not mastered outcomes. |

Continuous symmetry extension remains concise: planar rotations R(theta) compose by adding angles modulo 2π; a finite table cannot enumerate this group. Group theory describes composition; topology/smoothness are extra structure. No claim that every symmetry automatically gives a conservation law, or that every data augmentation is a group action. The next route lesson is **Partial Differential Equations, Conservation & Boundary Conditions**, with its own prerequisite readiness; do not invent a dependency from algebra to PDE.

## Topic-native representation contracts

These are selected for distinct hurdles, not a required component count. They can be consolidated if the complete mechanism remains visible.

1. **Composition on the actual square.** Fixed coordinate axes and numbered positions, distinct movable A/B/C/D markers, two explicitly labeled lanes “s then r” and “r then s,” with intermediate states visible. Step buttons, selected move, inverse and reset; table entry follows the selected row-after-column contract. Draw the true integer-coordinate quarter turns/reflections; don't animate a reflection by an unexplained rotation. Exact destination table is available. Preserve geometric outline separately from data labels so an unchanged outline does not falsely imply an unchanged input.
2. **Group element versus configuration figure.** An eight-node small Cayley diagram alongside a two-state opposite-mark action diagram, using named generator arrows and explained loops. Clearly state left/right multiplication for the Cayley arrows; calculate edge destinations from that convention. No copied diagram with reversed edge semantics.
3. **Orbit collection and fixed-point tally.** Paint four vertices with two/three bounded colors; show unique configurations once and list all transformations reaching each one. Selecting a transform exposes its vertex cycles and fixed-color constraints. A compact tally calculates Burnside's numerator and divisor. Do not collapse distinct colors, treat colors as permutable unless explicitly adding that different group, or identify reflection with rotation implicitly. Default binary task, changed ternary state reveals chiral pairs merged by reflection. Large enumerations stay bounded and are summarized, not hundreds of tiny illegible cards.
4. **Cosets and representative independence.** A small row/block picture groups the eight elements by rotations versus reflections; collapse to two orientation states. In a nonnormal counterexample, changing e to s within the same input block causes different output blocks under multiplication by r. Keep both representatives and the products visible. This is an operation-consistency investigation, not decorative colored blocks.
5. **Two routes through an equivariant map.** The same four square sensor channels appear before/after a chosen transformation and map. Tie matrix cells by self/neighbor/opposite relation, reveal each output's source contributions, and show exact route differences. Controls select raw, rotation-only or full-square-symmetric weights; change bounded rational coefficients/readings and reset. State the input/output action, units, and row-column convention. A separate scalar pooled output contrasts invariance. For arbitrary matrices, matrix commutators prove the all-input finite-group result; zero defect for one constant vector does not certify the map.
6. **Optional modular multiplication grid.** Highlight the map x→ax modulo n, collisions, units and all solutions to ax=b. A modulus-5 versus modulus-6 view reveals why division is sometimes legal. Display actual integers, not floating residual tolerances. An ordinary small table may serve better than another full lab; make that choice during the reading pass.

All drawings are derived exact finite examples except explicitly labeled smooth illustrative interpolation during optional motion. Readable phone geometry, responsive stacked comparisons and exact tables take priority over displaying the whole desktop grid at microscopic size. Color has text/shape labels. Manual keyboard steps, visible focus, live result summaries and deterministic resets are required; no autoplay is essential.

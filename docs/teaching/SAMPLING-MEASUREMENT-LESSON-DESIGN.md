## Learning route and conceptual hurdles

### 1. Decide what the number is supposed to mean

Open with two classrooms reporting many quiz rows. Does the larger file provide the fairer comparison? Before formulas, identify the person/class receiving the teaching method, a delayed-retention score measured at a specified time, and the population to which the statement refers. Introduce **estimand** after “the exact quantity we want”: a mean over declared units, a proportion, or a difference between two specified interventions. Student-weighted and classroom-weighted means can answer different questions even with the same rows.

Place a compact two-stage figure beside the explanation: target population → available frame → selected/enrolled units; the selected units then branch by assignment into methods A/B and finally yield recorded outcomes. A separate measurement arrow transforms the intended quantity into a recorded value. Sampling changes who is represented; assignment changes who receives which condition. Neither arrow silently validates the other. An administrative dataset is already the result of collection choices.

First checkpoint: a random assignment within one volunteer class supports which finite comparison, and which population claim still needs an argument? Feedback must name both questions rather than simply label the study “biased.”

### 2. Measure the intended quantity, not just a convenient proxy

Define outcome, operational rule, instrument/rubric, unit and timing. Use an original reference-value diagram: readings fluctuate around 12 while the reference is 10. More repeats stabilize the wrong center. Distinguish repeatability, a systematic offset and whether the measured construct answers the question. An immediate quiz and delayed retention are different outcomes; a highly repeatable proxy need not be valid for the intended construct.

Introduce calibration as a comparison, not magic removal of all error. If an estimated calibration correction is shared by every subsequent reading, its uncertainty is shared too. Keep a fixed unknown offset `b` distinct from a random variable representing uncertainty about a correction. Explain that NIST Type A/Type B classify evaluation methods, not random/systematic error. The beginner route does not become a metrology-certification guide.

### 3. Freeze a population; enumerate what sampling can change

Use eight fixed values `[2,4,6,8,10,12,14,16]`, population mean 9 and finite-population variance `S²=24` with denominator `N−1`. A sample is a subset; a sampling design assigns probabilities to those subsets; an estimator is a rule applied to each possible sample. The resulting distribution is over repeated applications of that rule to the fixed population. This is distinct from the histogram of the eight individual values.

Define SRS without replacement by **every size-n subset having the same probability**, not merely equal first-order inclusion. Derive `E(I_i)=n/N`, `E(I_i I_j)=n(n−1)/(N(N−1))` for distinct units, then the negative indicator covariance and

`Var(mean)=(1−n/N)S²/n`.

Show hand substitutions for n=2 (variance 9), n=4 (variance 3) and n=8 (zero sampling variance). Relate the equivalent denominator-N convention explicitly. A census removes this component of uncertainty under the fixed-frame/exact-measurement model; it does not remove coverage gaps, nonresponse or measurement problems. The n=1/N=1 boundaries need a direct interpretation rather than dividing by an undefined `N−1`.

Show a four-unit incomplete frame from the same eight: its census mean is 5, four below the target mean, even with zero remaining sampling variance. No decreasing-error picture may imply that additional observations from this unchanged frame remove its coverage bias. Distinguish target error, design bias and design variance; derive MSE = variance + bias² for a stated target and squared loss.

### 4. Unequal chances require matching contributions

Begin with a selected unit representing more than itself. Define inclusion probability `pi_i=P(unit i appears at least once)` under the full design. For positive known `pi_i` and fixed finite `y_i`, derive `E(I_i y_i/pi_i)=y_i`; summing yields an unbiased total, and dividing by a **known fixed** N gives an unbiased mean. Independence between inclusion indicators is not required for this expectation identity. Variance depends on the joint design, not just first-order probabilities.

Original exact fixture: four values `[2,4,8,10]`; the six size-two subsets in lexicographic order have probabilities `[4,2,1,1,1,1]/10`. Inclusion probabilities are `[7/10,3/5,2/5,3/10]`. The naive sample mean averages to 5 instead of the target 6; the HT mean averages to 6 and has variance `832/63`. Normalizing by the realized sum of inverse weights gives expectation `27571/5005`, not 6. This makes the bias/variance tradeoff visible without claiming the high-variance unbiased estimator universally wins. A single extreme estimate is not a failure of the expectation theorem.

Keep first-order inclusion distinct from per-draw selection probability and frequency weighting. For with-replacement sampling, explain why duplicates and `1−(1−p_i)^n` matter; a short exact program can compare the distinct-unit and per-draw estimators after defining their contracts. If any target unit has zero inclusion probability, the inverse formula cannot recover its unknown value. Nonresponse is another mechanism and does not become known positive inclusion merely by assigning a weight.

### 5. Choose a grouping that matches the collection constraints

For the eight-unit population, sample two from each half and combine stratum means with population shares. Enumerate 36 outcomes: expectation 9, variance `5/6`, compared with SRS n=4 variance 3. Derive the independent-stratum variance as a sum of weighted variances. Follow with unequal stratum sizes so learners cannot copy equal weights; the population share, not a convenient sample share, defines the target.

Contrast strata (sample within every stratum) with clusters (select some groups, then all or some units within them). State the role of a primary sampling unit and conditional second-stage probabilities. Do not claim every cluster design is less efficient: the correlation, estimator, allocation and cost matter.

Use a random-start systematic example `[0,10,0,10,0,10,0,10]`, step 2. Every unit has inclusion probability 1/2, but only two samples are possible: all zeros or all tens. The sample mean is unbiased over the two random starts, with variance 25, while SRS n=4 variance is `25/7`. Thus periodicity causes high sampling variance in this randomized fixture, not automatically design bias. A fixed convenient start can also introduce bias; identify the changed design. This is the concrete counterexample to “equal inclusion implies SRS.”

### 6. New units and repeated readings are different operations

Introduce the explicit model `Y_gj = theta + b + U_g + e_gj`, with independently generated unit effects, independent reading noise, zero means and finite variances. Identify what is fixed and what is redrawn. For G independent units and m readings per unit, derive

`Var(grand mean)=sigma_U²/G + sigma_e²/(Gm)`.

With variances 4 and 1 and 16 total readings, compare G=1,m=16; G=4,m=4; G=16,m=1. The uncertainty contributions are 65/16, 17/16 and 5/16; a fixed bias b remains in all three. Equal-correlation design effect `1+(m−1)rho` is a consequence of this balanced, equally weighted model compared with independent readings, not a universal effective-sample-size formula. Cost can differ between obtaining another unit and taking another reading; a bounded integer budget comparison must state that cost model.

Use the incoming `A=10+S+e_A`, `B=20+S+e_B` example with its actual distinct targets. Their average estimates a mean centered at 15; the paired difference is centered at 10 and cancels S. These are not automatically alternative estimators of one quantity. Explicitly subtract declared known offsets before constructing a common-target comparison. Pairing helps a difference when the covariance is positive under that model; it can hurt with negative covariance or fail through carryover/misaligned units. Estimating the covariance from iid pairs is separate from repairing clustered or selected data.

### 7. Assignment creates a different distribution

Give each synthetic enrolled unit two potential outcome entries; explain that only the entry for its assigned condition is observed in an actual experiment. The complete table is visible in the teaching model because it was constructed, not because real studies reveal both outcomes. State consistency, treatment versions and no interference before using a unit-level contrast.

For six baselines `[0,1,5,6,10,11]` and an additive effect 2, enumerate all 20 balanced three-treated assignments. Show the observed outcomes and realized difference in means for one allocation, then the whole assignment distribution. Its expectation is 2 and variance `203/15`. Use a heterogeneous-effect variation to show unbiasedness is not restricted to constant effects. Derive expectation from treatment indicators; do not assert group independence in a fixed-total assignment.

A design-based deeper derivation can establish `Var(tau_hat)=S_1²/n_1+S_0²/n_0−S_tau²/N` from the earlier indicator identity. Explain why the individual-effect variance is not observed and why omitting it yields a conservative variance estimator in expectation when both group sample variances exist. This is not a finite-sample Normal interval guarantee. Avoid using this extension before the beginner has interpreted the assignments.

Contrast random sampling with random assignment again: the latter identifies the enrolled-unit average under the specified conditions, while generalizing to a target population needs selection/transport assumptions. Allocation concealment prevents prediction of forthcoming assignments; blinding reduces measurement/behavior differences. Neither makes missing outcomes ignorable. A fixed seed reproduces an allocation; it does not establish fairness if one searches seeds until results look favorable.

### 8. Block variation and expose interactions

Pair adjacent baselines in the six-unit fixture, randomly selecting one treated unit per pair. There are eight assignments; averaging the three within-pair contrasts still targets 2, with variance `1/3`. The exact comparison explains why these **prognostic** blocks help. A changed pairing of unlike baselines must be available to show that grouping alone is not a guarantee. Block on pretreatment information and weight blocks for the declared estimand; do not construct groups after seeing favorable outcomes.

Then teach an interaction through a complete synthetic 2×2 table: `mean(A,B)=10+2A−B+4AB`, A/B each 0 or 1. Cells are 10,12,9,15. A's effect is 2 at B=0 and 6 at B=1; difference-in-differences is 4. Explain why an OFAT path that never observes the fourth combination cannot determine that interaction. The averaged effect of A is 4 under equal weighting across B, not an intrinsic constant under all target mixtures. These are declared synthetic means, not noisy estimates from one replicate.

A compact whole-plot diagram shows a condition assigned to a furnace run or classroom and multiple readings beneath it. Replication for that condition occurs at the run/class level. A short extension can explain why repeating readings does not supply the absent assignment replication. Domain-specific split-plot analysis remains a link, not an unexplained formula.

### 9. A complete protocol and an honest uncertainty plan

Preserve the original two-teaching-methods capstone, now with an explicit example acceptable protocol: enrolled class/student definition; declared delayed-retention outcome and rubric; interference-sensitive assignment unit; pretreatment blocking; concealed recorded assignment; measurement timing and blinded grading when feasible; unit IDs and repeated-reading IDs; missingness/exclusion/stopping rules; target weighting; and a reproducible assignment table. The synthetic program must not recruit, contact or randomize actual people.

Include a concrete finite-population precision target by solving the variance inequality for n under the stated S²/N and enumerating integer choices. Explain why that calculation does not fix bias or determine power without an effect, test, error levels and outcome model. Distinguish a design selected before outcomes from a revised analysis after seeing them.

Show missing-outcome bounds under a declared outcome range. With assigned A recorded as `[8,9,missing,missing]`, B `[4,5,6,7]`, and scores in [0,10], A's full mean ranges from 4.25 to 9.25 while B's is 5.5; the assigned-group contrast ranges from −1.25 to 3.75. The observed A average 8.5 alone does not determine the sign of the full assigned-group contrast. These are bounds for the missing assigned outcomes, not by themselves bounds for an unobserved causal effect. A missingness model would add assumptions; report them.

Close the actual module by linking to Ordinary Differential Equations & Linear Systems, explaining the transition from planning observations to modeling change over time. Relevant review links to inference, causal identification and domain experiment topics remain available without pretending they come next in the module.
